> “Occasionally, people with altitude sickness become irritable. It's rare, but that's one of the symptoms too.”

I had wanted to go to Europe with my mother, but I simply couldn't take enough time off. We decided to climb Mount Fuji instead. Fuji was close enough for a quick trip.

Some friends had climbed it, so it wasn't hard to imagine doing it myself. I didn't think it would be that difficult. On family trips, I'd been made to hike in places like Yosemite and Qixing Mountain in Taiwan. I'd even been to Bryce Canyon, some 2,600 meters above sea level.

I bought hiking shoes and got everything ready. And yet, for no particular reason, I stayed up until about two the night before. I was probably more excited than I realized.

We booked a Hato Bus tour. My mother is of a generation that likes organized tours, so that's often how we travel together. When I travel with friends my own age, we have to plan everything ourselves and keep things running once we're there. A tour is wonderfully easy by comparison.

When I think of Hato Bus, I picture a tour attendant: a woman, about my age or a little older. And, above all, a yellow bus. Come to think of it, is Hato Bus connected to Hato Sabure, those pigeon-shaped biscuits? Even the colors are similar.

I imagined a Mount Fuji tour would attract an older crowd. I'd be fine. I was young.

6:50 a.m., Shinjuku Station. Anyone dressed for hiking at that hour had to be on the same tour. I followed them through the maze to our meeting point outside the Cocoon Tower. A few people were gathering in front of the yellow bus.

As it turned out, our attendant was twenty. And a man. Instead of a yellow uniform, he wore sportswear. He was still finding his feet with the formal language of the job, and his inexperience showed. So young. Later, I learned that he would be climbing with us.

Most of the group were in their twenties or thirties: parents and children, groups of three friends, couples. About thirty people altogether. I was among the younger ones.

Here's how the two-day schedule was supposed to work. We would begin climbing at 1 p.m. and reach the eighth station at 4:30. There we would rest until 11 p.m., then make our push for the summit. We'd watch the sunrise at five and be back down by ten. After that, we'd wash off the sweat at a hot spring near Lake Kawaguchi.

Two guides accompanied us. Both looked experienced, though they seemed about my age. One had climbed Fuji more than a hundred times. That was reassuring. Looking back, having them there was a very good thing. They taught us much more than just how to climb the mountain. Tours are great, I thought.

We started at one. With about thirty people walking in two lines, we were told that the front would be easier and the back harder. Neither my mother nor I felt particularly confident, so we went straight to the front.

The pace was surprisingly slow. That was part of the technique, apparently. One step a second, gently upward. The clouds moved fast. The air was cool.

We stopped roughly every thirty minutes. We were probably taking much more care than we would have on our own. As the guide put it:

“Save your strength. There's a long way to go.”

Past the sixth station, the trees began to disappear. Looking back, we could see Fujiyoshida and the area around the lake. But the weather kept changing. A view that had been clear a moment ago would suddenly fill with cloud.

We reached the seventh station around four. We were behind schedule; the new plan was to reach our hut at six. There were too many climbers. About two thousand, according to our guide. As an aside, he told us that around the time Fuji became a World Heritage Site, there had been days with ten thousand.

Unfortunately, the path only grew narrower from there. We kept getting stuck in bottlenecks. But we were lucky enough to see Fuji's shadow. It happened to be a full moon, and its light cast the mountain's silhouette onto the clouds. Our guide said it was his first time seeing it in a hundred climbs.

Breathing matters on a mountain. Altitude sickness, the great enemy of climbing, comes down to a lack of oxygen. “Breathe out as though you're blowing out a candle,” they told us.

To be honest, I think I had a mild case. My head had hurt since around the seventh station. It felt like a migraine, and when I got my breathing right, the pain would ease. I told my mother it was probably my sinuses acting up in the cold. It really could have been. Unlikely, but not a lie. Still, I knew I'd stayed up late, and I knew I hadn't slept enough.

I thought that admitting it was altitude sickness would make my head hurt even more. So I kept telling my mother it wasn't. Her fussing over me was getting on my nerves, too. She'd tell me to put another layer on, then five minutes later tell me to take one off. I was irritable.

Business is often compared to climbing a mountain. For startups, there are guides like [this one](https://review.foundx.jp/entry/startup-fit-journey-v2): which mountain to climb, how to climb it, and so on.

I could see the resemblance. My head hurt all the time. That was exactly like the days I spent with my head in my hands at work. The advice, too, was to keep going steadily. Let other people pass you; climb at your own pace. That lesson hit home—in a very physical way.

And I was irritable all the time. That was familiar too. Things often go wrong. Often, you can't see the top. Your companions don't necessarily agree with you. But when you look back, you can see how far you've come.

Above all, you keep facing forward while everything around you changes. And you keep telling yourself: I'm probably still fine. I'm young. I can work as many hours as it takes.

We reached the hut at the eighth station at six. Our 11:30 p.m. departure was apparently still on. We wolfed down the curry that seems to be a Fuji-climbing tradition and went to bed shortly after seven.

By then, I wanted to go home. My head hurt. Even going to the toilet wasn't straightforward. I sweat a lot, which made it hard to stay at a comfortable temperature. When we stopped, I got cold and wanted another layer. When we moved, I got hot and started sweating. There was, of course, no hot spring. Water was precious, and no matter how much you sweated, there was only so much to drink.

But I couldn't just go down. I couldn't leave my mother on her own, and I'd inconvenience everyone else. Besides, I'd been told that if I turned back from there, I'd have to make my own way home. My headache got worse.

After barely sleeping the night before, I slept soundly for about two hours. It was already approaching ten. I went outside to use the toilet. Just beyond the building, two people were lying on a bench wrapped in silver emergency blankets. You couldn't just turn up and get a bed. There were a few people like that. Everyone was there for the sunrise.

Before long, it was time to gather and start climbing again. In the dark, headlamps were compulsory. Looking up the mountainside, their lights formed a line.

Above the eighth station, there were more rocks. More chances to fall, and little to see behind us in the dark. For someone with a mild fear of heights, it wasn't pleasant. The air grew thinner, the crowd thicker. We kept moving without knowing where we were.

“That torii gate is the summit!”

When the guide said it, I could see the gate. So that was the top. But the trail around the tenth station was narrow and terribly crowded. There was nowhere to stop, so we had gone nearly an hour without one of our usual half-hourly breaks. I was tired. I wanted it to end.

There was no great sense of release when I reached the summit. I rushed to the toilet. In that stinking cubicle, all I could think was how much it hurt my head to strain.

What struck me about the summit was that nowhere else in the world could Cup Noodles look so good. The white wisps of steam rising from the cups were a feast for the eyes. Not the taste, not the smell, not even the noodles themselves. That steam was everything.

The sunrise at five was magnificent. The sky was clear, and we had a beautiful view of the lakes. Sunlight emerged above the clouds. People raised their cameras.

A moment later, though, I was thinking only about getting down. We'd been told it would take five hours. Far too long.

On the way down, we practically ran. The pace was unbelievable. It hurt. It never seemed to end. It was supposed to take five hours, but it took about six.

“Which part was the hardest?”

Our attendant stood at the front of the bus and asked us. I raised my hand for the descent. Honestly, all of it had been hard, but the descent was hard.

Apparently, we'd been exceptionally lucky. The weather was good, the sunrise beautiful, and we'd seen Fuji's shadow. Most of all, every one of the thirty or so people had finished. Normally, about five would have to leave the group because of altitude sickness.

I sank into the hot spring I'd been looking forward to more than anything. Wonderful. There was air. My head still hurt a little, but I was back. I propped my feet against a rock in the outdoor bath, looked at the view, and took a deep breath.

As the air reached my brain, something occurred to me. I'd climbed a mountain, but nothing had changed. It had felt just like work. By a strange—and rather sad—coincidence, I like work.

Climbing Mount Fuji was “work.”

There was one small difference. I never wanted to climb Mount Fuji again.

I remembered what the guide had said when we finished, back at the fifth station.

> “Well done, everyone.
>
> […]
>
> “I suspect some of you will be back here next year. The memory of how hard it was will fade, and the good memories will bring you back.
>
> “I look forward to seeing you here again.”

Deals fall through. Applications get rejected. Work isn't all good either. But I don't remember any of it. Remind me and the anger might come bubbling back, but those things are just old toys packed in a box in a corner of my mind. I take them out over drinks, perhaps.

The good days, though, stay with me. Delivering a project. Finally getting something finished. I will probably remember those moments for the rest of my life.

When I think back, I don't want to climb again. But I do want to see that steam rising from the Cup Noodles one more time.

If it's “work,” I suppose that's how it goes. You don't want to, and yet you take your seat. And there you are, opening your laptop again.
